Camera Placement Study

Where should the cameras go on Nanyang Crescent opposite Hall 15, which way should they point, and how many are worth installing? We ran the simulator for every candidate layout against the same animal crossings and counted how many serious hits (a car touching an animal at more than 30 km/h) each layout prevents compared with having no system at all.

3,788 simulation runs · 120 single-camera layouts · 1 to 4 cameras · 4 scenarios · the demo's lens (110°, 35 m) · the same seeds give the same results every time

This is an early study, done with the presentation demo's own settings: one car at a time, fast animals and a quick driver. The later Installation Study re-did the placement question with values from research, cars both ways, and area coverage, and reached a different answer (two cameras in crossfire). Keep this one for the history and the method; use that one for the recommendation.

What this study found
Position Next to the hotspot, on the Hall 15 side

The best single camera stands on the south verge at x 550 and looks north, across the road and up the wildlife verge. It prevents 77% of serious hits. Any camera within about 20 m of the hotspot (x 400 to 700) that looks across the road prevents 60 to 77%. At x 850 the best is 21%.

Direction Look across the road, not along it

Facing north from the south verge (or south from the north verge) puts the whole crossing in view before the animal reaches the lane. Looking along the road from the same spot drops to 21 to 45%.

How many Two

One camera prevents 77% of serious hits, two 85%, three 87%, four 90%. The second camera adds 8 points for about 6 s more per trip; the third and fourth add 2 to 3 points each.

How the study worked

  • We used the demo's own simulation code, with the drawing switched off, so the results match what the demo shows.
  • Every layout faced the same 200 animal crossings per run, arriving in groups as in the demo, three in four from the wildlife verge. Each layout was compared with the same crossings and no system.
  • Each camera drives a warning sign placed where its view of the driver's lane begins. The warned driver stops at a line before the camera's view while the sign flashes, as in the demo.
  • The headline is the share of serious hits prevented, averaged over four scenarios:

Position and direction: one camera

We tried one camera at five positions along the road, in three mounting spots (a gantry over the centreline, the north verge, the south verge), facing eight compass directions. The grid shows the best mounting spot for each position and direction.

Serious hits prevented (%) by one camera, averaged over the four scenarios. Rows are positions along the road; the hotspot is at x 490. N means facing the wildlife verge. Darker green is better.

What the grid shows

  • The three positions around the hotspot (x 400, 550, 700) do well in any direction that covers the road. x 250 only works facing east, toward the hotspot, and x 850 does not work at all.
  • Across beats along. From x 550, facing north gives 77% and facing south 65%; facing west along the road gives 60% and facing east 21%. A camera looking across sees the animal on the grass before it steps out; one looking along the road sees it only when it is near the lane.
  • Diagonal directions (NE from x 400, NW from x 700) are nearly as good as straight across, so the exact aim is not critical.

The mounting spot matters a little

Best result per mounting spot. At the hotspot the Hall 15 side is best: from there the camera looks across the road and up the wildlife verge, where three in four animals come from.

How many cameras

Starting from the best single camera, we kept adding whichever camera helped most, up to four. We also tested three layouts designed by hand. The finalists were re-run with 10 seeds.

Recommended layout (from this study)

Two cameras at the hotspot (shaded): one on the Hall 15 side at x 550 looking north across the road and up the wildlife verge; one on the wildlife side at x 400 looking south across the road. Between them they see an animal on either verge before it steps out, from either direction of travel.

Why this pair works

  • Each camera looks across the road, so an animal is in view while it is still on the grass: 73% of crossings are seen before the animal reaches the road, against 57% for one camera.
  • The two face opposite verges, so crossings from either side are caught early, and each covers the lane the other sees at an angle.
  • At the hotspot it prevents 99 to 100% of serious hits; where crossings can happen anywhere, 68 to 73%. Its worst scenario (68%) is better than the single camera's (61%).
  • The cost: about 12 s per trip for the warned driver, because the signs flash more often and the car waits at the line. One camera costs 6.5 s.
  • If budget allows one: the Hall 15-side camera at x 550 facing north, 77% (worst scenario 61%).

Does the lens or the sign distance change the answer?

We re-ran the best layout for each number of cameras with four lenses, and with the sign visible from 320 units instead of 480.

Serious hits prevented (%), averaged over the four scenarios. The 110°/250 lens is far ahead at every camera count; the other three lenses are within noise of each other. Sign distance makes little difference once the camera sees the verge early.

What changed since the first run

  • The best single camera moved from the centreline to the verge. The first run's winner (x 400 centreline facing east) scored 46% on near misses. With a driver who needs time to react and can only see 21 m, what matters is seeing the animal before it steps out, which a camera looking across the road from the verge does and a camera looking along the road does not.
  • The recommended number fell from three to two. Verge cameras cover more each, so the third adds only 2 points.
  • The lens matters far more than before. The 110°/250 lens is worth 35 to 40 points over the 84°/150 lens on a single camera.
  • The demo's camera is not the best one. The demo keeps its centreline camera at x 590 facing west for a clear picture of "a camera watching the crossing".

What this study cannot tell you

  • It was run with the demo's settings, not research values: animals 5 to 8 times faster than life, a driver who reacts in 0.5 s, three in four animals from the wildlife verge, and a 110° lens that is not a real product. The Installation Study fixes all of these, and its placement answer is different.
  • One car at a time per direction, so there is no queuing and no delay beyond the driver's own wait.
  • The cameras never miss an animal in their view. Rain, glare and bushes are not modelled.

How to run it again

Open research/index.html in the project and press Run study. It runs offline in about 6 minutes and offers a CSV of every result. The numbers in this report come from research/results/camera-study.csv.
