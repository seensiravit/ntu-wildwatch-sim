Simulation Settings

This page explains what the simulator does and every setting behind it, in the order things happen each frame: animals arrive and decide whether to cross, the cameras see them, the signs flash, the driver sees, decides and brakes, and the outcome is scored. It covers both ways the simulator is used: the demo (the presentation pages, paced to be watched) and the research runs (values taken from published studies, used for the installation recommendation).

The map

The whole map is 1000 × 700 units; 1 unit is about 14 cm, so the road on screen is about 140 m long. Nanyang Crescent runs left to right with two lanes. The wildlife verge is at the top, Hall 15 at the bottom. Animals start in the shaded bands and walk across near the hotspot. The demo's camera sits on the centreline at x 590 looking west; its two signs stand beside the road at the camera. The dashed amber line is where a warned eastbound driver stops.
  • Speeds are in units per second; 100 units/s is 50 km/h. The road is 84 units (11.7 m) wide with two 42-unit lanes.
  • Eastbound cars drive the north lane (y 359) from x −40 to x 1040; westbound cars drive the south lane (y 401) the other way. One lap at 50 km/h takes about 11 s.
  • The crossing hotspot is centred at x 490 with a spread (standard deviation) of 60 units: about two thirds of animals cross within x 430 to 550.

What happens each frame

The screen shows the same road twice: on the left without WildWatch, on the right with it. Every animal exists as a pair of twins, one on each side, with the same start point, speed, boldness and timing, so the two sides only differ because of the cameras and signs. Sixty times a second (or in fixed 1/60 s steps in the research runs):

1
New animals arrive

from a random schedule (below), as twins on both sides.

2
Animals move

and, when they reach the roadside, decide whether to cross, wait or turn back, looking at the cars on their own side.

3
Cameras look

and the signs linked to them switch on or off (right side only).

4
Each car looks, decides and moves

using its headlights, the signs it has seen, and the car in front.

5
Outcomes are scored

serious hit, low-speed contact, near miss or safe pass, once per car and animal.

When and where animals arrive

Animals come in groups, and groups arrive at random with no pattern, which statisticians call a Poisson process: the time until the next group is a short minimum plus a random extra drawn from an exponential curve. Each group is one species, chosen by weight; its size is one plus a random count. Everything is drawn from a seeded random generator, so the same seed replays the same run exactly.

What an animal does at the roadside

An animal walks straight toward the road. When it is 25 units from the edge it decides once: cross, wait, or turn back. First it checks whether a car is a threat: a car coming toward its crossing point within a set distance, or a car stopped near the crossing point. Then, in order:

  1. No threatening car: cross.
  2. The animal never noticed the car (each species has a chance of not noticing): cross.
  3. Bold enough: cross anyway (a boar dashes faster than it walks).
  4. Less bold: wait at the roadside, shown as three pulsing dots. It crosses once no car has threatened it for 1 s. If it has waited its species' maximum, it turns back instead.
  5. Otherwise: turn back, shown as ↩ and counted as "turned back".

Once on the road an animal keeps walking and no longer reacts to cars. (Real pangolins freeze on the road; the model does not do this.) The chances come from the response types in Jacobson et al. 2016: macaques avoid, boar dash, pangolins pause. Each group shares one boldness value; each animal rolls its own "did I notice" value.

Cameras and signs

  • A camera sees every walking animal inside its view cone. Detection is perfect inside the cone: nothing is missed and there are no false alarms. Rain, glare and bushes are not modelled.
  • The demo's camera has a 110° view and a 250-unit (35 m) range. The research runs use 90°, the widest real thermal lens.
  • A sign flashes while any camera linked to it sees an animal (optionally only while the animal is on the road, and optionally for a few seconds after). It switches off at once otherwise.
  • A sign's warning zone on the driver's path starts 480 units (67 m) before the sign, where the driver can first see it, and ends where the cameras' coverage of the lane ends. Its stop line is 20 units before the coverage begins.
  • The demo has two signs at the camera facing both directions. The research installation has one sign per direction, placed where the cameras' coverage of that lane begins.
  • The left side of the screen has none of this: no camera, no signs, no zone.

What the driver can see

The driver has a 40° headlight beam. An animal is noticed once it is on the road within the headlight range ahead, or on the grass inside the beam. Once noticed it stays noticed until the car has passed it. For each noticed animal the driver keeps two clocks: time since noticing it, and time since it entered the car's own lane.

At 50 km/h with a 1.5 s reaction the car needs about 35 m to stop, more than it can see. An animal first seen in the lane at the edge of the light gets hit. This "outdriving the headlights" is the problem the system is built to fix: the camera sees the animal on the grass before the driver can.

How the driver decides, every frame

The driver works out a target speed and a braking strength from every rule that applies. The lowest target speed and the strongest braking win. In order of priority:

1
Reaction time

Nothing changes until the reaction time has passed since the animal was noticed. In the last 0.2 s the foot moves to the brake and the car coasts.

2
Warned by a sign

A driver who has seen a flashing sign stays warned until the end of that sign's zone. What they do depends on the rule: wait at the stop line until the sign has been off for a moment (giving up after 20 s in the demo, 10 s in research), stop for a fixed time, or just slow to 20 km/h and go through. In the research runs only 30% of drivers follow the rule; the rest slow by 7 km/h while the sign flashes. Every warned driver reacts faster and looks further.

3
Animal in the car's own lane

Stop 15 units short of it, braking a little harder than strictly needed at first and easing off, never beyond the car's limit. If even that cannot stop in time, the car hits the animal.

4
Animal elsewhere on the road

Slow to a speed from which the car could still stop if the animal stepped across, but not below 20 km/h. (A study setting makes the driver stop for any animal on the road instead.)

5
Animal on the grass near the kerb, lit by the headlights

Ease off to 40 km/h.

6
Otherwise

Cruise at 50 km/h.

The braking is applied smoothly: it builds up at a limited rate (gentle, firm or emergency) and releases quickly, so the speed curves look like real driving. After a stop the car waits 0.3 s once clear, then pulls away. In the demo both cars start every lap together, 1.5 s after the slower one has left, so they meet the same animals.

Traffic and car-following (installation demo and research runs)

The presentation demo has one car per side. The installation demo and the research runs release a stream of cars in both directions, so queues and waiting can be measured.

  • Arrivals. Cars arrive at random in each direction, a 2 s minimum gap plus an exponential extra: about one every 45 s per direction in the research runs (80 cars an hour), every 20 s in the installation demo. Each car has its own cruise speed (50 ± 4 km/h), so faster cars catch slower ones. Both sides of the screen get the same cars.
  • Getting on. A car enters only when there is room behind the last car; otherwise it waits off the map and the wait counts as delay.
  • Following. A standard car-following model (the Intelligent Driver Model): keep a 2 m gap plus 1.0 s of travel, brake harder the shorter the gap, up to 2.0 m/s². A follower notices the car ahead braking only after 0.5 s, and cannot see an animal hidden behind it. Every other driving rule still applies.
  • Westbound cars use the south lane with their own warning zones.
  • What is measured per car: delay against an empty road, stops, time stopped, time at the stop line, time waiting to get on. Per side: queue length, how much of the time the sign is on with no animal on the road, and far-lane hits (an animal hit in the second lane it entered).

Scoring

Encounter
opens when a walking animal is in the car's lane within 150 units ahead; the car's speed at its closest approach is the one that counts.
Hit
the car body touches the animal, at any speed, whoever moved into whom. Above 30 km/h it is a serious hit; otherwise a low-speed contact, which includes an animal walking into a stopped car.
Near miss
no contact, but the car was faster than 35 km/h at its closest.
Safe pass
no contact and the car was at 35 km/h or slower by then.
Turned back
an animal that gave up at the roadside because of a car.

Each animal is scored once per car per lap. If an animal already counted as a near miss or safe pass touches the car later, it moves to hits. The scoreboard tiles, the DED dashboard, the scenario card and the research pages all read these counters.

Why there are two sets of values

Demo values (index.html, the scenario)

Paced for a two-minute presentation: animals 5 to 8 times faster than life, a group every 12 s, a 0.5 s driver, one car per side, a 110° lens. Right for showing the idea; not for predicting an installation.

Research values (installation.html, the studies)

Every number taken from published studies where one exists: animal speeds and behaviour, driver reaction and braking, seeing distances, traffic volume, sign compliance, lens. Cars both ways with following. The installation demo runs these at 2× speed so the slow animals are watchable. The Installation Study report lists each value's source and how solid it is.

In the code the demo values are the base configuration (js/config.js) and the research values are a block (config.research) laid over them by the research runs and the installation page; the demo itself never applies them.
