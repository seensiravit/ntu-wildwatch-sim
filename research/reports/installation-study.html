Installation Study

If WildWatch were installed on Nanyang Crescent, where should the camera go, what should drivers do when they see an animal, and what should the sign ask of them? This study fixes everything else at values from the literature (animal speeds and behaviour, driver reaction and braking, traffic, lens) and varies only those three things, with a stream of cars in both directions.

The answer

Serious hit = contact above 30 km/h; counts are per 100 animals that reached the road. Runs put a group of animals on the road once a minute so crossings stay independent; costs are reported per crossing event (driver-seconds of extra delay) and per serious hit prevented. At the real rate of about 0.3 crossings an hour and 80 cars an hour the delay per car is hundredths of a second.

What was fixed, and from where

The demo keeps its presentation pacing; the research runs apply these values on top (config.research). Confidence: measured = a study reports it; estimated = derived from related measurements; guess = no data. Full citations are in the project README.

How the study worked

  • Each run is the demo's own simulation, headless, for 10 simulated minutes: two worlds share the same animals; the OFF world has no camera or signs; the ON world has the camera and one sign per direction at the edge of its coverage, seen 67 m ahead.
  • Cars arrive both ways as Poisson streams (a car every 45 s per direction on average, 50 ± 4 km/h), follow each other, and queue behind a car at the line. 30% of drivers follow the sign rule; the rest slow by 7 km/h while the sign flashes. Every warned driver looks further (45 m instead of 30 m) and reacts in 1.0 s instead of 1.5 s, which is what field studies of warned drivers show.
  • Three variables: the driver's own policy on seeing an animal (never brakes / slows / stops for anything on the road), the sign rule for complying drivers (no stop; stop 5 or 10 s; wait until the sign is off, plus 0.5, 2 or 5 s), and what makes the sign flash (any animal in view, or only one on the road). 300 seeds per setting, intervals about ±2 points.
  • Placement: every single-camera position × mounting spot × facing (120), three two-camera layouts, and today's camera, for a 90° and a 60° lens, 300 seeds each.

    Reading it

      Conclusion for each setting

      One line per setting: what was tried, what the numbers said, and what to do. "Serious hits" are per 100 crossings on the ON side; the unwarned road has 26.

      Recommendation

      Recommended installation

      Top-down, 1 map unit ≈ 0.14 m. Two thermal cameras with 90° lenses and 35 m range: one on the Hall 15 verge at x 400 facing north-east, one on the wildlife verge at x 550 facing south-west. Between them they see both verges and both lanes of the crossing. A sign for each direction where their combined coverage of that lane begins (eastbound at x 300, westbound at x 650), visible 67 m ahead, flashing while any animal is in either view, asking drivers to slow. The dashed camera is the optional third one (x 400, wildlife verge, facing east), which lifts the corridor in view to about 93%.

      Limits

      • A quarter of unwarned crossings ending in a serious hit is the model's exposure with 1 m/s animals, 1.5 s reactions and a car every 22 s on average across both directions; it is a comparison baseline, not a forecast of collisions.
      • The share of drivers who stop at a flashing sign is not reported in any field study; 30% is an estimate, and the sensitivity rows show the answer does not depend on it.
      • Pangolins freeze on the road in reality (a "pauser"); here they only pause at the roadside. Macaque night crossings would cluster at dusk and dawn. The hotspot's position and width are not measured.
      • Detection is perfect inside the camera's view; no weather, glare or occlusion. Poisson car arrivals suit light night traffic; peak-hour platoons would raise queue lengths.
